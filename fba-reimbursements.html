Amazon FBA Reimbursements: How to Find and Claim the Money You're Owed

Every FBA seller hands Amazon their inventory and trusts it to be counted, stored, and shipped correctly. Most of the time it is. But units get lost in fulfillment centers, damaged on the shelf, short-counted when a shipment arrives, or refunded to a customer who never sends the item back. When that happens, Amazon owes you money. The catch: it doesn't always pay on its own, and the window to ask is short.

Where the money usually slips away

  1. Lost or damaged in the warehouse. Units that go missing or get damaged while Amazon is holding them.
  2. Short inbound shipments. You sent 100 units, Amazon checked in 94. The missing six are easy to overlook.
  3. Customer returns that never came back. The customer got a refund, but the item was never returned, or came back and was never put back into your sellable stock.
  4. Fee overcharges. Amazon measures or weighs an item wrong and charges you a higher fulfillment fee on every sale.

Why sellers miss it

Amazon now reimburses many warehouse losses automatically, which leads a lot of sellers to assume everything is handled. It isn't. Automatic payments can be missing, partial, or valued lower than they should be, and returns and inbound shortages often need you to open a case yourself. Claim windows are also limited, often just a couple of months, so old losses quietly expire. Check Seller Central for the current deadlines on each claim type.

How to check your own account

  1. Open your Reimbursements report in Seller Central to see what Amazon has already paid you.
  2. Compare it against your Inventory Ledger for units marked lost, damaged, or disposed.
  3. Check customer returns for refunds where the item never came back or wasn't restocked.
  4. For recent inbound shipments, compare units sent against units received.
  5. Anything lost or damaged with no matching reimbursement is money to claim. Open a case with the details and your evidence.

When Amazon says no

Denials are common, and many are wrong. A clear, calm reply that points to the exact report, the unit count, and the date usually does better than starting over. Keep it factual: what happened, what the reports show, and what you're asking for.
